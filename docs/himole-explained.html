How do you teach a giant AI new skills without making it brittle?

1. The problem: acing the practice test, flunking the real one

Imagine a student who prepares for an exam by memorizing ten years of past papers. On questions that look like the past papers, they're brilliant. Then the examiner changes the style, and the student falls apart, because they learned the patterns of the questions instead of the subject.

Machine learning people have names for these two situations. Questions that look like the training material are called in-distribution (ID). Questions from the same subject but in an unfamiliar style or source are out-of-distribution (OOD). A model that holds up on both is called robust.

The paper starts with an uncomfortable experiment. Take a large language model, fine-tune it on biomedical text (a task called named entity recognition: finding disease names, drugs, and so on), and measure it both ways. Try it yourself:

This is the punchline of the whole paper. On unfamiliar biomedical text, the tuned model scored lower than the untouched model. The standard way of teaching a model new skills actively made it worse at the thing it was supposed to be learning, just phrased differently. HiMoLE exists to fix this.

To understand the fix, you need two ingredients first: what "tuning" actually is, and what a "team of experts" means for a neural network. Both are simpler than they sound.

2. First ingredient: LoRA, the sticky-note method

A large language model is like a printed encyclopedia with billions of entries. Retraining the whole thing for a new specialty would mean reprinting every page: slow, expensive, and risky. So instead, you freeze the book and add sticky notes with small corrections and additions. That's LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation).

Here's the tiny bit of structure worth knowing. The model's knowledge lives in huge grids of numbers called weight matrices. LoRA doesn't touch them. It adds a correction that is deliberately skinny: instead of one huge grid, it learns two thin strips, called A and B, which multiply together to fake a full-size correction. The strip thickness is called the rank, written r. Skinnier strips mean fewer numbers to learn.

LoRA is cheap and it works, which is why everyone uses it. But look back at section 1: that failing model was a LoRA model. One set of sticky notes gets stretched across every kind of example in the training data, so it averages everything together and clings to surface patterns. The next idea tries to fix that by using several sets of notes.

3. Second ingredient: a team of specialists, and a dilemma

If one set of sticky notes gets muddled trying to cover everything, why not keep several sets, each specializing in one kind of material, and pick the right one for each input? That's a mixture of experts: the "experts" are just separate LoRA modules, and a small learned component called a router decides which experts handle which input.

The interesting question turns out to be: what should the router look at when it decides? Existing methods answer it in one of two ways:

  • Word-by-word (token routing). The router picks experts separately for every word, based on that word alone. Like a mail room that reads each word of a letter and forwards each one somewhere different.
  • Whole-sentence (sentence routing). The router skims the entire input once and picks experts for all of it. Like a receptionist who reads the whole letter, then hands it to one department.

The paper measured both, and found a genuine trade-off:

Why does this happen? Think about the student again. Word-level details are like the exact phrasing of past exam questions: extremely useful until the phrasing changes, then misleading. Whole-sentence meaning is like actually understanding the subject: less precise on any one question, but stable when the style shifts. Statisticians call this a bias-variance trade-off; you can just think of it as detail versus stability.

So: details win at home, stability wins away. The obvious wish is to have both at once. That wish is HiMoLE.

4. The big idea: two routers that must agree

Picture a hospital. Patients aren't assigned to a doctor by a clerk who reads their file one word at a time, and they aren't treated by the front desk either. Two decisions happen in order: triage reads the whole case and picks a department, then inside that department, specific specialists take on the details.

HiMoLE builds exactly this into the model. The experts are organized into N departments the paper calls Knowledge Competition Groups (KCGs), and each department contains M specialists, the Knowledge Collaboration Experts (KCEs). Each specialist is a small LoRA module, the sticky notes from section 2. With N = 3 and M = 4 you get 12 experts arranged in a 3 × 4 grid.

Two routers score them:

  • The sentence router skims the whole input (technically: it averages the word representations into one summary) and gives each department a score. Three departments, three numbers.
  • The token router looks at one word at a time and gives every individual expert a score. Twelve experts, twelve numbers.

Then comes the one clever move the whole paper turns on: multiply the two. Each department's sentence score is stretched across its four specialists and multiplied into their word scores. Multiplying acts like the word AND: if either the sentence view or the word view says "irrelevant" (a score near zero), the product is near zero, and that expert stays quiet. An expert fires only when the big picture and the fine detail both point at it. Finally, only the top 2 experts of the 12 actually run.

Try the tricky sentence and look at the word "doctor". On its own, that word screams Medicine, and a word-only router would send it to two medicine specialists. But the sentence is about a match, so the sentence router boosts Sports, and the final pick becomes one medicine specialist plus one sports specialist. That's the whole trick: the stable big-picture view vetoes the misleading detail without erasing it.

Names make sense now, too. Departments compete with each other for inputs (so unrelated material stops interfering, which the paper calls negative transfer), while specialists inside one department collaborate on shared material. Hence Knowledge Competition Groups and Knowledge Collaboration Experts.

The actual equations, for the curious

One summary per sentence, made by averaging word representations:

hsen = average_pool(htoken)

Each router is a single learned matrix producing scores. Gsen has N entries (departments), Gtoken has N×M entries (experts):

Gsen = Wsen · hsen    Gtoken = Wtoken · htoken

The gate: broadcast-multiply (⊙), squash into probabilities (softmax), keep the top k = 2:

Ghie = KeepTopK( Softmax( Gsen ⊙ Gtoken ) )

The layer's output is the frozen original plus the weighted expert corrections:

o = W0 · htoken + Σi Ghie(i) · Ei · htoken

The dimension subtlety implementers get wrong: Gsen has N numbers, Gtoken has N×M, so the ⊙ stretches each department score across its M experts before multiplying. This layer replaces the feed-forward block in every transformer layer of the model.

5. Training it: why the order of steps matters

There's a chicken-and-egg problem hiding in this design. The router can only learn to send inputs to the right department if the departments are already different from one another. But the departments only become different if the router sends them different inputs. Start everything from scratch at once and you get twelve identical, interchangeable experts and a router with no reason to prefer any of them.

6. Two house rules during training

In stage 2, the model is scored on three things at once: how well it does the actual task, plus two extra penalty terms that keep the team of experts healthy. Both penalties guard against a failure mode you can see coming from the hospital analogy.

Rule one: don't let one specialist take all the patients

Left alone, routers develop favorites. A slightly better expert gets more inputs, which trains it more, which makes it better, until one expert does everything and the other eleven atrophy. The load-balancing penalty gets larger the more lopsided the assignments become, so training is nudged toward spreading the work. Drag the slider to feel its shape:

Penalty value

1.00 perfectly even (the minimum)

The penalty is exactly 1.00 when all four experts share the work equally, and climbs toward 4 as one expert hoards everything. The optimizer keeps trying to pull it back down.

Four experts shown for clarity, using the paper's Switch-style penalty with the simplification that assignment shares equal router probabilities.

Rule two: don't let the departments become clones

The other failure is quieter: two departments could drift toward producing the same output, and then you've paid for twelve experts and gotten six. The diversity penalty measures how similar the departments' outputs are, as an angle: outputs pointing the same way are clones; outputs at right angles are doing genuinely different jobs. Training pushes the similarity down.

Similarity (what the penalty averages)

0.91 nearly clones

1.00 means identical direction, 0.00 means fully independent jobs. The penalty is the average similarity over every pair of departments; lower is better.

In the paper this is cosine similarity between normalized department outputs, sampled every ten layers to keep it cheap. Removing it costs a small but statistically significant amount of performance.

The full training objective, for the curious L = Ltask + α · Laux + β · Ldiverse

Ltask is the ordinary language-modeling loss. Laux = N · Σi Fi Pi, where Fi is the fraction of words sent to expert i and Pi its average router probability. Ldiverse is the mean pairwise cosine similarity between normalized department outputs. Stage 1 runs at learning rate 3e-4; stage 2 at 3e-5.

Every tuned method gains about 20+ points on familiar data, and every earlier method pays for it on unfamiliar data, landing below the untouched model. HiMoLE is the only one that takes the full gain and climbs back to the untouched model's level on unfamiliar text. It doesn't magically beat the base model away from home; it stops the damage while keeping the win.

The paper checks the idea from a few more angles:

From the paper's Figure 4, Table 2, and Appendix E. The typo test corrupts characters in the input; whole-sentence context barely changes, so the sentence router keeps routing sensibly while word-only routers get dragged around by the noise.

Honest fine print, because the Feynman method demands it: the gains vary by domain (bigger in knowledge-heavy fields like biomedicine, smaller in general text), OOD scores remain below ID scores for every method, and the whole comparison is at one fixed parameter budget. HiMoLE narrows the gap; it does not abolish it.

8. Your turn: explain it back

Feynman's actual method has a step most people skip: after learning something, try to teach it, notice where you stumble, and go back. Try answering each of these out loud before you peek.

Why did ordinary fine-tuning make the model worse on unfamiliar text?
Fine-tuning rewards whatever reduces error on the training data, and surface patterns (phrasings, formats, local word statistics) reduce it fastest. The model leans on those patterns, and they're exactly what changes when the data source changes. Like the student memorizing past papers, it traded understanding for pattern-matching.
Why multiply the two routers' scores instead of adding them?
Multiplication behaves like AND: if either score is near zero, the product is near zero, so an expert needs approval from both the whole-sentence view and the word-level view to fire. Addition behaves like OR: a huge word-level score could single-handedly activate an expert even when the sentence context says it's irrelevant, which is precisely the failure being fixed.
Why can't you skip stage 1 and just train everything together?
Chicken and egg. A router learns by noticing that some experts handle certain inputs better, but freshly initialized experts are all equally mediocre, so the router gets no signal, so the experts never specialize. Stage 1 breaks the loop by force: cluster the data by meaning, train each department alone on one cluster, and only then let the router learn on top of genuinely different departments. Skipping it cost up to 31.9 F1 points.
What would go wrong without the two extra penalties?
Without load balancing, a rich-get-richer loop concentrates all the work in one or two experts and the rest waste their capacity. Without the diversity penalty, departments drift into producing near-identical outputs, so you pay for N departments and effectively get fewer. Both penalties protect the same asset: genuinely different specialists worth routing between.

The whole paper in five sentences

  1. Fine-tuning a big model with LoRA works great on familiar data but can make it worse on the same subject from an unfamiliar source.
  2. Splitting the added capacity into routed experts doesn't fix it, because word-by-word routing overfits to details while whole-sentence routing is stable but coarse.
  3. HiMoLE uses both: a sentence router picks a department, a token router picks specialists inside it, and their scores multiply so an expert fires only when both views agree.
  4. It's trained in two stages: first each department specializes alone on one cluster of the data, then everything trains together with penalties against lopsided workloads and clone departments.
  5. The result keeps all of fine-tuning's gains on familiar data while climbing back to the untouched model's level on unfamiliar data, where every earlier method fell below it.

If you can retell those five sentences in your own words, you understand the paper. That was the deal.
